Sometimes the right decision leaves someone worse off. A project needs to stop. An organization needs to change. A commitment can no longer be sustained.

Delay can leave people investing more time in work with no future, or allow a manageable problem to become a crisis. Leadership requires a willingness to act despite those consequences.

But establishing that a change is necessary does not settle how to carry it out. The announcement, the transition, and the support we provide determine a substantial part of what happens next. They belong in the reasoning that makes the decision defensible.

How we handle that work also gives people evidence about our character. They learn what our commitments mean when keeping them becomes difficult, and whether they can rely on us the next time their interests are at stake.

> A necessary outcome can still be reached through unnecessary harm.

## You do not get to delegate the weight

The person who passes judgment should swing the sword.[^sword]

The authority to impose a consequence comes with a responsibility to confront it. You should not get to exercise the power while arranging for someone else to carry all the discomfort.

You can ask someone to coordinate a transition. You can rely on people with the right expertise to carry it out. But you still owe the people affected an explanation you are willing to give yourself. You still have to own the consequences, including the ones that make your decision harder to defend.

“I made the decision; someone else can deal with the people” is not a complete account of leadership. Distance can remove precisely the information that should inform our judgment: what the plan actually asks of someone, and where it leaves them.

The minute you start delegating away the hard part—not because someone else is better equipped to do the work, but because you do not want to face it—you start dying as a leader.

## Be precise about what was necessary

“We had no choice” deserves a more specific question: no choice about what?

Ending an investment? Acting before a deadline? Giving people little information? Leaving them to work out the consequences themselves?

Consider a project that no longer warrants its investment. Continuing would consume resources needed elsewhere. Stopping it may be the right call.

That does not tell us when to inform the team, how to represent their work, or how to handle commitments made to customers. It does not tell us what support people need to move to their next responsibilities. Those choices face their own constraints and need their own justification.

The standard cannot be that nobody is upset. Someone may understand a decision and still strongly oppose it. Their disappointment does not establish that the decision was wrong; their acceptance does not establish that it was handled well.

*Crucial Conversations* calls the supposed choice between honesty and respect a “Fool’s Choice.”[^crucial-conversations] We can be clear about what has been decided without dismissing what it costs the person hearing it. A gentle but evasive answer can leave someone unable to plan.

Support costs money. A transition takes time. Some information cannot yet be shared. We should weigh those constraints before approving the change, with a dependable standard for how people will be treated. Calling the overall decision necessary does not make every choice about its execution inevitable.

## It should never be easy

It should never be easy. It may get easier.

Experience should make you more capable of acting under pressure. You may become less anxious before a difficult conversation, more precise in your explanation, and better able to listen without becoming defensive. You may stop confusing your discomfort with evidence that the decision is wrong.

Nobody benefits from a leader making a painful event about their own feelings. You can become steadier without becoming numb. Someone else's loss should still carry moral weight, even when you are certain about the decision.

In *Meditations* 5.20, Marcus Aurelius describes turning an impediment into material for action. In 6.30, he puts kindness alongside disciplined action.[^marcus] I take those ideas together: the difficult conversation is part of the work of leadership. Here, the obstacle to overcome is our temptation to avoid responsibility, not the person affected by our decision.

That gives us something to practice: explaining the reasoning, hearing an angry response without retaliating, and correcting a consequence we could reasonably have prevented. Composure helps us do that work. Indifference gives us permission to skip it.

## Character is in the choices

Character shows in what we choose when we have room to choose differently.

It is relatively easy to speak well of people while their work serves our needs. The more demanding test comes when our interests diverge. Their contribution does not disappear because their project, role, or agreement no longer fits our plans. People should not have to remain useful to us to be treated with care.

Epictetus argues in *Discourses* 2.18 that our actions strengthen the corresponding habits.[^epictetus] Each decision is practice for the next one. Facing consequences and arranging not to face them are both things we can become practiced at. Experience alone does not tell us which we are learning.

Those choices become evidence for other people, too. Do we keep an inconvenient commitment? Acknowledge an error? Apply a standard consistently when the circumstances are comparable? People see whether our account of ourselves survives contact with a difficult situation.

A colleague can dislike my decision and still have reason to trust my conduct. Earning that trust requires more than being agreeable. It requires judgment they can rely on and commitments that hold under pressure.

## Resolve is not hostility

A leader can end an argument by making disagreement unpleasant. That does not establish that the decision improved. Even when aggression secures a concession, the concession does not account for what happened to cooperation or the information available next time.

James Detert and Ethan Burris found that managerial openness was associated with employees speaking up with suggestions for improvement, with perceived safety helping explain the relationship.[^employee-voice] My concern is the reverse incentive: when bringing a problem means inviting a confrontation, silence can become the easier choice. A meeting with fewer objections may contain less information.

In three experiments, Christine Porath and Amir Erez found that rudeness reduced task performance and helpfulness.[^rudeness] Contempt can carry an immediate cost to the work. I want the person who sees a flaw in the plan to bring it forward while we still have time to act.

We can set demanding targets, negotiate firmly, stop failed investments, and terminate employment when the reasons warrant it. Listening does not give someone a veto; correcting an error does not mean abandoning the goal. The work still has to meet the requirement.

A successful outcome is not proof that every part of the method was necessary. Could we achieve the result while preserving more of the information and cooperation the next result will require?

## Play the long game

A business needs capable people who will choose to join, stay, and do difficult work together. Losing people can disrupt execution; attracting replacements depends on more than having a vacancy. Research connects turnover with organizational performance and recruiting outcomes with the job, the organization, and how candidates experience the process.[^talent] I take those findings as a reason to include the people needed for continued execution in the business case for change.

Enjoying our colleagues matters, but trust asks more than whether we like them. Roger Mayer, James Davis, and David Schoorman's model distinguishes ability, benevolence, and integrity: can you do the work, do you care about my interests, and do you follow principles I can accept?[^trust-model] A demanding colleague can earn that confidence while making decisions I dislike.

Kurt Dirks and Donald Ferrin's meta-analysis linked trust in leadership with commitment, performance, and lower intention to quit.[^leadership-trust] That last measure is an intention, not an observed departure. Pay, workload, growth, and the work itself still matter. Trust belongs among the conditions that make staying worthwhile; it cannot stand in for all of them.

A layoff makes this concrete. Joel Brockner and colleagues found worse reactions among remaining employees who identified with departing colleagues and perceived the departing employees' compensation as inadequate.[^layoff-survivors] Removing an expense leaves a team that still has to believe it can work effectively together.

Adam Grant's *Give and Take* describes how generosity can build trust and reputation, and how givers can burn out or be exploited without boundaries.[^give-and-take] The longer horizon includes both what others learn from our conduct and what we practice through our choices. That is the practical sense in which I think about karma: we carry those relationships and habits into the next decision.

Decency deserves no less consideration when its return is uncertain. Integrity cannot guarantee commercial success. It can help preserve conditions the business needs to succeed, while meeting obligations that matter in their own right.

## Finish the decision

Before approving a consequential change, we should be able to explain who bears the cost, which consequences are unavoidable, and what we will do about the harm still within our control. Where a responsible approach costs more or takes longer, that belongs in the proposal.

For a reduction in staff, I would want a plan for the business that remains: what work stops, which commitments continue, and who has the capacity to meet them. The plan should specify how people will be told and what transition support is reasonably possible. Someone needs the authority and resources to carry that work through.

Then judge the result beyond the announcement. Did the savings materialize after transition costs? Can the remaining team meet its commitments? Are important risks still raised early? Are we losing effective people the plan depended on retaining? Retaining everyone is not the objective; sustaining the capabilities the business needs is.

We must remain available to hear consequences we failed to anticipate and correct what we can. An explanation that was defensible at approval does not relieve us of responsibility for how the plan works in practice.

I want us to make the changes the business needs without needlessly damaging its ability to succeed. That means meeting the moment and the goal: making the hard call, carrying it out with integrity, and taking responsibility for what follows.

**How you do it is part of the decision. Being willing to face what you decided is part of the job.**

[^crucial-conversations]: **[Crucial Conversations: Tools for Talking When Stakes Are High](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/mhp/product/crucial-conversations-3e.html)**  
    Joseph Grenny, Kerry Patterson, Ron McMillan, Al Switzler, and Emily Gregory · Third edition, 2021

    The “Fool’s Choice” rejects treating honesty and respect as incompatible; see also [Crucial Learning’s glossary](https://cruciallearning.com/glossary/). This supports the distinction between candor and disrespect, not a guarantee that painful decisions can leave relationships unchanged. Applying it to the responsibilities around a decision is the argument made here.

[^give-and-take]: **[Give and Take: A Revolutionary Approach to Success](https://adamgrant.net/book/give-and-take/)**  
    Adam Grant · 2013

    Grant describes both the long-term value of trust and reputation and the risk of burnout or exploitation. His [Wharton interview](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/givers-vs-takers-the-surprising-truth-about-who-gets-ahead/) discusses those mechanisms and the importance of boundaries. This informs the long-game argument; it is not evidence that every act of generosity earns a return.

[^marcus]: **[Meditations, Book Five, §20](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.5.five.html)**  
    Marcus Aurelius · Translated by George Long

    In 5.20, an impediment to action can become material for an appropriate response. [Book Six, §30](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.6.six.html) places kindness and social duty alongside disciplined action. The application to difficult leadership conversations is mine, not a claim that Marcus wrote about modern employment decisions or promised commercial success.

[^epictetus]: **[Discourses, Book Two, Chapter 18](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/Book_2/Chapter_18)**  
    Epictetus · Translated by George Long

    Actions maintain and strengthen their corresponding habits; Epictetus specifically warns that repeatedly acting in anger reinforces anger. This is the philosophical basis for the character-formation argument, not empirical evidence of a financial return or a doctrine of guaranteed reward and punishment.

[^employee-voice]: **[Leadership Behavior and Employee Voice: Is the Door Really Open?](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2007.26279183)**  
    James R. Detert and Ethan R. Burris · 2007

    A two-phase field study in a restaurant chain linked managerial openness with improvement-oriented employee voice, with perceived psychological safety mediating the relationship. This is evidence about speaking up, not a randomized test of leadership styles or a direct estimate of company profits. The author's [full-text copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255576931_Leadership_Behavior_and_Employee_Voice_Is_The_Door_Really_Open) is also available.

[^rudeness]: **[Does Rudeness Really Matter? The Effects of Rudeness on Task Performance and Helpfulness](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2007.20159919)**  
    Christine L. Porath and Amir Erez · 2007

    Three experiments found reduced routine and creative task performance and reduced helpfulness under rude treatment. The findings concern rudeness in experimental settings, not firm standards, lawful terminations, or a quantified effect on long-term business performance.

[^layoff-survivors]: **[Survivors' Reactions to Layoffs: We Get By with a Little Help for Our Friends](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/survivors-reactions-to-layoffs-we-get-by-with-a-little-help-for-o/)**  
    Joel Brockner, Steven Grover, Thomas Reed, Rocki DeWitt, and Michael O'Malley · 1987

    The strongest negative reactions occurred when survivors identified with those laid off and perceived the departing employees' compensation as inadequate. Outcomes were work performance in a laboratory study and organizational commitment in a field study. The result supports considering treatment of departing colleagues, not a universal severance amount, a guaranteed return on support, or a claim that all layoffs have identical effects.

[^sword]: **[A Game of Thrones](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/108336/a-game-of-thrones-by-george-r-r-martin/)**\
    George R. R. Martin · 1996, Bran's first chapter

    The sentence adapts Ned Stark's maxim about passing sentence and swinging the sword. Its application here concerns accountability, not literal punishment. The opening image evokes a different story: in Cicero's [Tusculan Disputations, Book Five, §§61–62](https://www.attalus.org/cicero/tusc5A.html#61), the suspended sword reveals the peril accompanying power. Neither metaphor establishes the essay's business claims.

[^trust-model]: **[An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335)**\
    Roger C. Mayer, James H. Davis, and F. David Schoorman · 1995

    This conceptual model distinguishes perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity as contributors to trustworthiness. Trust involves willingness to accept vulnerability; it is distinct from liking, cooperation, or a guaranteed outcome.

[^leadership-trust]: **[Trust in Leadership: Meta-Analytic Findings and Implications for Research and Practice](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/675/)**\
    Kurt T. Dirks and Donald L. Ferrin · 2002

    The meta-analysis draws on 106 samples. It reports associations with outcomes including commitment, job performance, and intention to quit. Intentions are not actual departures, and the findings do not establish that integrity causes retention or financial success through the entire chain proposed here.

[^talent]: **[Turnover Rates and Organizational Performance: A Meta-Analysis](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/turnover-rates-and-organizational-performance-a-meta-analysis/)**\
    Tae Youn Park and Jason D. Shaw · 2013

    Turnover rates were negatively associated with organizational performance on average, with differences by turnover type and context. This does not establish that every departure harms performance or that necessary terminations should be avoided.

    Derek S. Chapman and colleagues' [Applicant Attraction to Organizations and Job Choice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16162065/) (2005) synthesizes 71 studies. Recruiting outcomes were associated with job and organizational characteristics, recruiter behavior, process perceptions, and perceived fit. Connecting these findings to how consequential decisions are implemented is the essay's inference, not a measured effect of a particular layoff on hiring.
