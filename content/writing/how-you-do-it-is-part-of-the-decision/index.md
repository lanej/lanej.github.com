Sometimes the right decision leaves someone worse off. A project needs to stop. An organization needs to change. A commitment can no longer be sustained.

Avoiding those decisions is not automatically compassionate. Delay can leave people investing more time in work with no future, or allow a manageable problem to become a crisis. Leadership requires a willingness to act despite those consequences.

But once we establish that a change is necessary, it is tempting to treat the rest as implementation: make the announcement, assign the follow-up, move on.

That draws the boundary too narrowly. How we carry out a decision determines a substantial part of its consequences. It also reveals our character: what we are willing to take responsibility for, whose costs we consider, and how far beyond the immediate outcome we think. Our responsibility is to achieve the goal while leaving a business capable of succeeding after this decision, too.

> A necessary outcome can still be reached through unnecessary harm.

## You do not get to delegate the weight

The person who passes judgment should swing the sword.

The point is not that a leader must personally perform every task that follows a decision. It is that the authority to impose a consequence comes with a responsibility to confront it. You should not get to exercise the power while arranging for someone else to carry all the discomfort.

There is a difference between delegating work and delegating away the hard part.

You can ask someone to coordinate a transition. You can rely on people with the right expertise to carry it out. But you still owe the people affected an explanation you are willing to give yourself. You still have to own the consequences, including the ones that make your decision harder to defend.

“I made the decision; someone else can deal with the people” is not a complete account of leadership.

The minute you start delegating away the hard part—not because someone else is better equipped to do the work, but because you do not want to face it—you start dying as a leader.

## Be precise about what was necessary

“We had no choice” deserves a more specific question: no choice about what?

Ending an investment? Acting before a deadline? Giving people little information? Leaving them to work out the consequences themselves?

Those are separate decisions. They may be subject to different constraints, and they need their own justification.

Consider a project that no longer warrants its investment. Continuing would consume resources needed elsewhere. Stopping it may be the right call.

That does not tell us when to inform the team, how to represent the work they did, or how to handle commitments made to customers. It does not tell us what support people will need to move to their next responsibilities.

The business case establishes why the project should end. It does not answer those other questions.

The standard cannot be that nobody is upset. Someone may understand a decision, receive a clear explanation, and still strongly oppose it. Their disappointment does not by itself establish that the decision was wrong. Their acceptance does not establish that it was handled well.

The more useful question is whether we took reasonable steps to avoid making a difficult situation worse. That might mean information early enough to act on, practical support, or a direct answer instead of false hope. A gentle but evasive answer can leave someone unable to plan.

*Crucial Conversations* calls the supposed choice between honesty and respect a “Fool’s Choice.”[^crucial-conversations] We can be clear about what has been decided without dismissing what it costs the person hearing it. Respect does not require ambiguity, and candor does not require contempt.

There are real constraints. Support costs money. A transition takes time. Some information cannot yet be shared. Those constraints should lead to explicit tradeoffs, not a general exemption from considering the people affected.

Some consequences are inseparable from the decision. But calling the overall decision necessary does not make every choice about how to carry it out inevitable.

## It should never be easy

It should never be easy. It may get easier.

Experience should make you more capable of acting under pressure. You may become less anxious before a difficult conversation, more precise in your explanation, and better able to listen without becoming defensive. You may stop confusing your own discomfort with evidence that the decision is wrong.

That is different from becoming indifferent to what the decision costs someone else. The aim is not to suffer enough to prove that you care. Nobody benefits from a leader making a painful event about their own feelings. You can become steadier without becoming numb.

In *Meditations* 5.20, Marcus Aurelius describes turning an impediment into material for action. In 6.30, he puts kindness alongside disciplined action.[^marcus] I take those ideas together: the difficult conversation is not standing between us and the work of leadership. It is part of that work. Here, the obstacle to overcome is our temptation to avoid responsibility, not the person affected by our decision.

There is an opportunity to practice honesty without contempt and resolve without indifference. The demand is not permanent distress; it is refusing to let another person's loss become morally trivial.

If the work gets easier because we have placed enough distance between ourselves and the people affected, we have removed part of what should inform our judgment.

## Character is in the choices

Character is not just whether we can explain a decision. It is what we choose when we have room to choose differently.

A company that genuinely cannot afford to help people through a transition faces a different problem from one that can readily afford reasonable support and chooses to provide none. When support is readily affordable, withholding it simply because people lack the power to demand it is a failure of character, not an unavoidable business necessity.

It is relatively easy to speak well of people while their work serves our needs. The more demanding test comes when our interests diverge. Their contribution does not disappear because their project, role, or agreement no longer fits our plans.

People should not have to remain useful to us to be treated with care. Nor should that care depend on access or negotiating leverage. A standard should be something people can count on without having to secure an exception for themselves.

Epictetus argues in *Discourses* 2.18 that our actions strengthen the corresponding habits.[^epictetus] That makes each decision more than a test of existing character. It is practice for the next one. Facing consequences and arranging not to face them are both things we can become practiced at. Experience alone does not tell us which we are learning.

## Resolve is not hostility

A leader can end an argument by making disagreement unpleasant. That does not establish that the decision improved. Even when aggression secures a concession, the concession does not account for what happened to cooperation or the information available next time.

James Detert and Ethan Burris found that managerial openness was associated with employees speaking up with suggestions for improvement; their analysis linked that relationship to how safe employees felt speaking up.[^employee-voice] My concern is the reverse incentive: when bringing a problem means inviting a confrontation, silence can become the easier choice. A meeting with fewer objections may contain less information, not better agreement.

There is also evidence of an immediate performance cost. In three experiments, Christine Porath and Amir Erez found that rudeness reduced performance on routine and creative tasks, as well as helpfulness.[^rudeness] These were bounded experiments, not measurements of an entire company's profitability. They nevertheless challenge the assumption that contempt is merely an unpleasant style with no operational cost.

Firmness asks whether the work meets the requirement. Hostility punishes the person who brings an unwelcome answer. I want the person who sees a flaw in the plan to bring it forward while we still have time to act.

We can set demanding targets, negotiate firmly, stop failed investments, and terminate employment when the reasons warrant it. Those actions need sound judgment and a willingness to follow through. Anger is not a prerequisite. Listening does not give someone a veto; correcting an error does not mean abandoning the goal.

A successful outcome is not proof that every part of the method was necessary. The useful question is whether we could achieve the result while preserving more of the information and cooperation the next result will require.

## Play the long game

The ethical failure matters on its own. It can also be shortsighted.

Adam Grant’s *Give and Take* describes how generosity can build trust and reputation over time, while also showing how givers can burn out or be exploited without boundaries.[^give-and-take] I take that as an argument for considering more than the immediate exchange—not a promise that every decent act will be repaid.

A layoff makes the longer horizon concrete. Reducing payroll may be necessary. But the business still needs the people who remain to exercise judgment and work together. Their reaction to how colleagues were treated belongs in the assessment of whether the change worked.

Joel Brockner and colleagues studied this in both a laboratory and a workplace. Survivors reacted most negatively when they identified with those laid off and perceived their compensation as inadequate: lower performance in the laboratory and reduced organizational commitment in the field study.[^layoff-survivors] That does not establish a universal severance formula. It does show why the effects of a departure cannot be assessed only through the expense removed.

Forgoing support saves an immediate, visible expense. The longer-term costs may be harder to count. Someone can believe the underlying decision was necessary and still reconsider how much trust to place in the organization that carried it out.

That is the practical sense in which I think about karma. Not a guarantee that the world will settle every account, but a recognition that our choices shape the relationships and habits we will live with. The inward consequence connects to Epictetus: what we repeatedly do becomes easier to repeat.

Playing the long game is not being decent as a tactic to extract more from people later. It is recognizing that the way we act now helps determine what will be possible between us later.

Integrity does not guarantee commercial success, and hostility does not guarantee failure. The business argument is narrower: avoid needlessly damaging the conditions on which execution depends. The ethical obligation does not disappear when the financial return is uncertain.

## Finish the decision

Making the call is only part of the work.

Before approving a consequential change, we should be able to explain who bears the cost, which consequences are unavoidable, and what we will do about the harm still within our control. Where a more responsible approach costs more or takes longer, that belongs in the proposal—not outside the calculation.

For a reduction in staff, I would want a plan for the business that remains: what work stops, which commitments continue, and who has the capacity to meet them. The plan should also specify how people will be told and what transition support is reasonably possible. Neither the operating plan nor the treatment of people should be an afterthought.

Then judge the result beyond the announcement. Did the savings materialize after transition costs? Can the remaining team meet its commitments? Are important risks still being raised early? Are we losing people the plan depended on retaining? Those are questions about whether the decision achieved its purpose.

The people carrying out the work need the authority and resources to do it. Their responsibility for execution does not replace ours for the decision. We must remain available to hear consequences we failed to anticipate and correct what we can.

I do not want a gentler version of a failing business. I want us to make the changes the business needs without needlessly damaging its ability to succeed. That means meeting the moment and the goal: making the hard call, carrying it out with integrity, and taking responsibility for what follows.

**How you do it is part of the decision. Being willing to face what you decided is part of the job.**

[^crucial-conversations]: **[Crucial Conversations: Tools for Talking When Stakes Are High](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/mhp/product/crucial-conversations-3e.html)**  
    Joseph Grenny, Kerry Patterson, Ron McMillan, Al Switzler, and Emily Gregory · Third edition, 2021

    The “Fool’s Choice” rejects treating honesty and respect as incompatible; see also [Crucial Learning’s glossary](https://cruciallearning.com/glossary/). This supports the distinction between candor and disrespect, not a guarantee that painful decisions can leave relationships unchanged. Applying it to the responsibilities around a decision is the argument made here.

[^give-and-take]: **[Give and Take: A Revolutionary Approach to Success](https://adamgrant.net/book/give-and-take/)**  
    Adam Grant · 2013

    Grant describes both the long-term value of trust and reputation and the risk of burnout or exploitation. His [Wharton interview](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/givers-vs-takers-the-surprising-truth-about-who-gets-ahead/) discusses those mechanisms and the importance of boundaries. This informs the long-game argument; it is not evidence that every act of generosity earns a return.

[^marcus]: **[Meditations, Book Five, §20](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.5.five.html)**  
    Marcus Aurelius · Translated by George Long

    In 5.20, an impediment to action can become material for an appropriate response. [Book Six, §30](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.6.six.html) places kindness and social duty alongside disciplined action. The application to difficult leadership conversations is mine, not a claim that Marcus wrote about modern employment decisions or promised commercial success.

[^epictetus]: **[Discourses, Book Two, Chapter 18](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/Book_2/Chapter_18)**  
    Epictetus · Translated by George Long

    Actions maintain and strengthen their corresponding habits; Epictetus specifically warns that repeatedly acting in anger reinforces anger. This is the philosophical basis for the character-formation argument, not empirical evidence of a financial return or a doctrine of guaranteed reward and punishment.

[^employee-voice]: **[Leadership Behavior and Employee Voice: Is the Door Really Open?](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2007.26279183)**  
    James R. Detert and Ethan R. Burris · 2007

    A two-phase field study in a restaurant chain linked managerial openness with improvement-oriented employee voice, with perceived psychological safety mediating the relationship. This is evidence about speaking up, not a randomized test of leadership styles or a direct estimate of company profits. The author's [full-text copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255576931_Leadership_Behavior_and_Employee_Voice_Is_The_Door_Really_Open) is also available.

[^rudeness]: **[Does Rudeness Really Matter? The Effects of Rudeness on Task Performance and Helpfulness](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2007.20159919)**  
    Christine L. Porath and Amir Erez · 2007

    Three experiments found reduced routine and creative task performance and reduced helpfulness under rude treatment. The findings concern rudeness in experimental settings, not firm standards, lawful terminations, or a quantified effect on long-term business performance.

[^layoff-survivors]: **[Survivors' Reactions to Layoffs: We Get By with a Little Help for Our Friends](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/survivors-reactions-to-layoffs-we-get-by-with-a-little-help-for-o/)**  
    Joel Brockner, Steven Grover, Thomas Reed, Rocki DeWitt, and Michael O'Malley · 1987

    The strongest negative reactions occurred when survivors identified with those laid off and perceived their compensation as inadequate. Outcomes were work performance in a laboratory study and organizational commitment in a field study. The result supports considering treatment of departing colleagues, not a universal severance amount, a guaranteed return on support, or a claim that all layoffs have identical effects.
